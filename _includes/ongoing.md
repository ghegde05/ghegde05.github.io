  <li>
    <strong>Opportunities for household cooling demand flexibility as infrastructure in Indian cities</strong><br>
    <span class="ongoing-meta"><u>Hegde G.</u>, Bhardwaj S., and Mathur V. · Transitions Research, Goa · working paper</span>
    <details>
      <summary>abstract</summary>
      <p>India's grid is failing in two directions at once. Renewable curtailment is rising sharply — over 8,100 GWh of solar was held back between April and June 2026, compared with 2.1 TWh in the previous financial year — because thermal plants cannot ramp down below technical minimums to absorb midday generation. Simultaneously, an increasingly inflexible evening peak is being built by residential cooling: each 1°C of warming adds roughly 7 GW to evening peak demand, projected to reach 12 GW per degree by 2030. Room air conditioners already draw an estimated 60–70 GW, close to a quarter of the national peak, trending toward 180 GW by 2035.</p>
      <p>India's policy response has been overwhelmingly supply- and infrastructure-led: capacity targets, transmission, utility-scale storage, smart metering and market reform. Demand-side instruments exist but are implemented only to a limited extent. Time-of-Day tariffs depend on a smart-meter rollout running well behind target; demand response remains voluntary, with utility-led pilots without national scale-up; consumer-sited storage has no support comparable to that on the utility side. The binding constraint is therefore not technical feasibility but adoption — whether flexibility can reach enough consumers, fast enough, for the system to absorb rising renewable capacity and rising demand together.</p>
      <p>This paper argues that residential cooling in India's small and medium cities, with warming temperatures, faster appliance adoption, and the construction of the largest share of the country's future floor area, promises opportunities for demand-side flexibility. Drawing on Indian evidence on appliances, building stock, and grid operations, we develop a feasibility framework that treats households as active infrastructure rather than passive loads, and examine how efficiency standards, building envelopes, distributed storage, tariffs, and automated appliances interact. The paper explores combination and sequencing to convert renewable capacity into a reliable 24×7 supply and proposes transition metrics for demand flexibility in rapidly urbanising economies.</p>
